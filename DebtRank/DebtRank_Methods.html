1 · The question DebtRank answers

Banks lend to one another. That is usually a good thing — it moves liquidity to where it is needed — but it also means that one bank’s problem can become every bank’s problem. If bank B loses money, then any bank A that has lent to B holds an asset that is now worth less, so A loses money too, which hurts A’s creditors, and so on. A shock travels through the web of interbank exposures the way a rumor travels through a group of friends.

The question DebtRank answers is:

How much of the banking system’s total equity is ultimately destroyed by a shock, once we account for all the rounds of contagion — not just the initial hit?

The answer is typically much larger than the direct loss. In the bundled 20-bank example, a shock that directly destroys about 16% of system equity ends up destroying about 71% after propagation — an amplification of more than 4×. Ignoring the network would understate risk by a factor of four.

Analogy. Think of the multiplier in introductory macroeconomics: an initial change in spending is amplified as it circulates through the economy. Here an initial equity loss is amplified as it circulates through the interbank network. DebtRank computes that multiplier — and tells you which banks contribute most to it.

2 · Balance sheets and notation

Consider n banks, indexed i = 1, …, n. Each bank’s balance sheet has two kinds of assets:

deposits, debt, interbank borrowing
Interbank assets Σj Aij

the owners’ buffer against losses

The central object is the exposure matrix A: the entry Aij is the amount bank i (the lender) has lent to bank j (the borrower). If j gets into trouble, it is i who is exposed and stands to lose.

Because equity is the buffer that absorbs losses, it is natural to track each bank’s health by its relative equity loss:

(1) hi(t)  =  [ Ei(0) − Ei(t) ] ⁄ Ei(0)

hi(t) is the fraction of bank i’s initial equity that has been wiped out by round t of the cascade. It runs from 0 (unharmed) to 1 (all equity gone — the bank is in default). Everything in the tool — every chart, ranking, and risk measure — is built from these hi(t).

3 · The leverage matrix

How much should bank i worry about bank j? The exposure Aij alone does not say: a $10bn exposure is existential for a small bank and a rounding error for a giant. What matters is the exposure relative to the lender’s equity buffer. This gives the interbank leverage matrix (Bardoscia et al. 2015):

(2) Λij  =  Aij ⁄ Ei

Λij reads: “for every dollar of equity bank i has, it has Λij dollars at risk with bank j.”

A two-bank example. Bank A has equity of 100 and has lent 50 to bank B, so ΛAB = 50/100 = 0.5. Suppose B loses 20% of its equity. Under the model’s accounting, A marks down its claim on B in proportion and loses 0.5 × 20% = 10% of its own equity. The leverage matrix is exactly the set of “pass-through” coefficients of the contagion process.

Why mark losses before default? Because a creditor’s claim on a weakened borrower is genuinely worth less today: the probability of being repaid in full has fallen. This mark-to-market view is what distinguishes DebtRank from default-cascade models (e.g. Eisenberg–Noe), where losses occur only when a borrower actually defaults — and it is why DebtRank detects fragility that default models miss.

4 · How distress propagates

The dynamics is a simple recursion. In each round, every bank passes its newly received distress on to its creditors, scaled by the leverage coefficients. The linear DebtRank rule (Bardoscia et al. 2015) is:

(3) hi(t+1)  =  min{ 1,  hi(t)  +  Σj Λij [ hj(t) − hj(t−1) ] }

Reading equation (3) term by term:

hi(t) — the damage bank i has already suffered; losses are never undone within the cascade.

Λij [ hj(t) − hj(t−1) ] — the new loss bank i takes because borrower j got worse this round. Using the change in hj rather than its level is essential: it prevents the same dollar of distress from being counted twice. Bank i has already marked down j’s earlier losses; only news moves the books.

min{1, ·} — equity losses are capped at 100%: once a bank’s equity is exhausted it defaults, and limited liability means it cannot lose more than everything.

The cascade starts from an initial shock hi(1) — for example, every bank losing α% of its external assets, or one chosen bank defaulting outright — and equation (3) is iterated until nothing changes any more.

5 · Original vs. linear DebtRank

The tool offers two propagation rules, and the difference is one assumption: how many times may a bank pass distress on?

Original DebtRank (Battiston et al. 2012)

Each bank propagates distress only once — the first time it is hit. Formally, each bank is in one of three states: Undistressed, Distressed (just hit; will transmit this round), or Inactive (already transmitted; absorbs further losses but passes nothing on). The motivation was to prevent shocks from bouncing back and forth along cycles in the network (A hurts B, B hurts A, …) and inflating the count.

Linear DebtRank (Bardoscia et al. 2015)

Each bank propagates every round in which it receives new distress, per equation (3). Bardoscia et al. show this is the rule consistent with period-by-period mark-to-market accounting; the difference form in (3) already prevents double counting, so no once-only restriction is needed.

Relationship. The original rule shuts down transmission earlier, so it always yields losses no larger than the linear rule: original DebtRank is a lower bound, linear DebtRank the more conservative (higher) estimate. Running both brackets the truth. In the tool the linear rule is the default.

6 · When do cascades die out?

Will the recursion in (3) converge, or can a tiny shock snowball? Away from the min{1,·} cap, equation (3) says the vector of new losses each round equals Λ times the previous round’s new losses. This is exactly like a first-order autoregression: in the AR(1) model yt = βyt−1 + ut, shocks fade away when |β| < 1 and explode when |β| > 1. With a matrix instead of a scalar, the role of |β| is played by the spectral radius — the largest eigenvalue modulus |λmax(Λ)|:

(4) |λmax(Λ)| < 1  ⇒  shocks are damped (stable)   ·   |λmax(Λ)| > 1  ⇒  shocks are amplified (unstable)

In the unstable regime even an arbitrarily small shock grows each round until the min{1,·} cap binds — that is, until banks start defaulting. The spectral radius is therefore a one-number health check for the whole network, and the tool reports it the moment data are loaded. The bundled example has |λmax| = 0.90: stable, but close enough to 1 that amplification is strong.

Policy reading. The spectral radius rises with interbank leverage. Deleveraging, higher capital (larger Ei), or smaller bilateral exposures all push |λmax| down — this is the model’s formal case for capital regulation.

7 · System-level risk: the global vulnerability H(t)

To summarize the whole system in one number, average the individual losses, weighting each bank by its share of total equity:

(5) H(t)  =  Σi ( Ei ⁄ Σj Ej ) hi(t)

H(t) is the fraction of system-wide equity destroyed by round t — like a capitalization-weighted index of damage. Two readings of the final value H(T):

Level: H(T) = 0.71 means 71% of the banking system’s equity is gone. Amplification: the ratio H(T) ⁄ H(1) compares the final loss with the direct first-round loss — the network multiplier of Section 1.

8 · Firm-level risk: impact and vulnerability

Two different questions can be asked about each bank. This tool uses the default-impact and scenario-vulnerability convention of Battiston et al. (2016), which is distinct from the single-bank shock experiment used for Figure 2 of Bardoscia et al. (2015).

Impact — “how dangerous is this bank to others?”

The DebtRank of bank i is found by a thought experiment: set hi(1) = 1 (bank i alone defaults), run the cascade, and measure the equity loss induced in the rest of the system (the seed bank’s own equity is excluded, since destroying it is not contagion):

(6) DRi  =  H(T | hi(1)=1)  −  Ei ⁄ Σj Ej

DRi = 0.37 reads: bank i’s default would destroy 37% of everyone else’s equity. This is the measure behind the phrase too central to fail: in the original 2012 application, mid-sized but highly connected institutions ranked above some larger, less connected ones.

Vulnerability — “how dangerous are others to this bank?”

Bank i’s vulnerability is simply its final loss hi(T) under the scenario you chose — how badly it is hurt when the shock hits everyone.

Relation to Bardoscia et al. (2015). In that paper’s Figure 2 experiment, one bank at a time receives the same small external-asset shock. Its impact is the total system loss in that experiment, while each bank’s vulnerability is its average loss across all of the single-bank experiments. The rankings displayed by this tool should therefore not be interpreted as a replication of Bardoscia’s Figure 2.
The impact–vulnerability map. Plotting impact (x) against vulnerability (y) splits banks into four groups. The upper-right quadrant — high impact and high vulnerability — contains the institutions that both amplify and attract trouble: the natural priority list for supervisors.

9 · Three rounds of losses & fire sales

Following Battiston et al. (2016), the tool decomposes the total loss into three economically distinct layers:

First round — the direct shock: each bank’s external assets fall by α%, so hi(1) = min{1, αAEi⁄Ei}. This is what a stress test without a network would report.

Second round — interbank contagion: everything added by iterating equation (3).

Third round (optional) — simplified fire sales: the tool assumes that each surviving bank sells a share of external assets proportional to its cumulative equity loss. Aggregate sales depress the market price; with a linear market impact φ, the price drop is

(7) Δp⁄p  =  φ × (assets sold) ⁄ (total external assets)

and every bank still holding those assets marks them down, losing Δp⁄p × AEi ⁄ Ei more equity. This channel needs no direct contractual link at all: two banks that have never traded with each other are connected through the prices of the assets they both hold.

Scope of the fire-sale extension. This is a transparent heuristic inspired by the third-round channel of Battiston et al. (2016). The published model derives each bank’s sale fraction from restoration of its initial leverage and applies the price shock to retained assets. The present tool does not solve that target-leverage equation, so its third-round results are illustrative scenario outputs rather than a replication of the paper’s fire-sale calibration.

10 · Loss distributions, VaR and CVaR

A single scenario gives a single number. To see the whole distribution of possible outcomes, the tool runs a Monte-Carlo experiment: draw a random shock size α from a Beta distribution (always between 0 and 1, with a mean you choose), apply it to all banks, run the cascade, record H(T) — and repeat thousands of times. Two summary statistics of the resulting loss distribution:

(8) VaR95  =  the loss not exceeded in 95% of trials   ·   CVaR95  =  the average loss in the worst 5% of trials

VaR (Value-at-Risk) is a quantile — the same concept as a percentile in any distribution. CVaR (conditional VaR, or expected shortfall) answers the follow-up question: when things are worse than the VaR, how bad are they on average? CVaR is always at least as large as VaR and is more informative about tail risk.

The headline result. Computing the distribution twice — once with first-round losses only, once with full propagation — shows the network’s signature: it shifts the entire loss distribution, and especially its right tail, sharply rightward. A risk manager using the no-network VaR would be severely underestimating capital needs.

11 · Reconstructing a network from aggregates

Bilateral exposures Aij are confidential almost everywhere; what is usually public is each bank’s total interbank lending and borrowing. The tool constructs one deterministic, reproducible bilateral approximation from these totals:

Step 0 — balance the margins. A closed interbank system must have equal total lending and borrowing. If the uploaded totals differ, borrowing is rescaled proportionally to equal total lending, following the adjustment described by Bardoscia et al. (2015). The tool rejects margins that cannot be represented without self-exposures.

Step 1 — directed fitnesses and a binary topology. Lending fitness xouti and borrowing fitness xinj are computed separately. Candidate links are ranked by the directed Bardoscia probability pij = z xouti xinj / (1 + z xouti xinj). The highest-probability links form a deterministic binary support. The base density is 5%; for small systems the tool applies an average-degree floor of three so that the network is not mechanically too sparse.

Step 2 — RAS (how large is each link). Starting on the selected support, rows and columns are alternately rescaled to match lending and adjusted borrowing. If the sparse support cannot reproduce both margins, the next-highest-probability links are added until RAS converges.

Analogy. RAS is the iterative proportional fitting used to fill in a contingency table when only its row and column totals are known. The result is the matrix consistent with the observed totals that stays as close as possible to the seed’s structure.
Retained simplifying assumption. Bardoscia et al. sample many random binary networks and summarize results across that ensemble. This tool intentionally keeps one deterministic topology so the HTML and Python versions are reproducible and directly comparable. It is therefore a fitness-informed approximation, not a complete replication of the paper’s reconstruction experiment.

Reconstruction adds model risk: the true network may be more or less concentrated than the deterministic approximation. Treat Format-B results as a sensitivity case, not as a bound.
